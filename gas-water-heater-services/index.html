A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. Most failures trace back to one of those parts. In Vallejo, CA, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Diagnosing a gas water heater properly means checking combustion, not just parts. Across Vallejo, our technicians measure thermocouple output in millivolts, study the pilot and burner flame for color and shape, read gas pressure at the valve, and test draft at the hood so we know exhaust is leaving the house. We check the combustion chamber for soot and scorching, which point to a blocked flue or poor combustion air, and we test for carbon monoxide near the heater. Keep one rule in mind: a gas smell means leaving the house and calling the gas utility from outside before anything else, and Vallejo Water Heater can take over from there.

Once we know the cause, the repair is usually simple. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • A tank that pops and bangs while it heats, a classic sign of sediment
  • Showers that go cold sooner than they did a year ago
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • Rust-colored hot water or rust streaks on the tank
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Start with the hazards. Every gas water heater involves fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust that contains carbon monoxide. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and replacing them one by one is slow and costly.

What a trained technician brings is certainty. We test instead of guessing, from thermocouple millivolt readings and burner flame checks to gas pressure at the valve and a draft test at the hood. Before we leave, every connection is leak-tested, the vent is confirmed to carry exhaust outside, and CO is checked. And because some gas heaters are not worth another repair, we give you a straight answer on repair versus replacement first, then handle either one with correct venting, gas piping, and the code items your installation requires.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here are our recommendations:

  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years and replaced when it is worn
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
